About  ·  who keeps this, and why

Hi, I’m Bhuvan.

I like tumbling down weird and wonderful rabbit holes. I like knowing random things, and I think there’s a certain joy in being a dilettante.

It’s not just me — everyone likes discovering new things, but for whatever reason, most people lose that ability to experience awe and wonder. It’s tempting to blame social media apps and smartphones for this pathology, but that’s too simplistic — they’re not the only reason people seem to have become incurious and, perhaps more tragically, okay with being so.

This blog is an attempt for me to continue slipping into weird rabbit holes and share them. The hope here is that whatever I share might trip fellow travellers on the internet and send them sliding down a rabbit hole.

The goal isn’t to be an expert on everything, but to maintain a sense of wonder about the world. Sometimes the most interesting insights come from wandering without a destination in mind.

Why a commonplace book

They were private.
The famous ones got published.

For a few hundred years, a reading person kept a commonplace book: a blank volume for the lines worth keeping. Not a diary — a diary is about your day. A commonplace book is about everything else. You copied out what struck you, filed it under a head you chose yourself, and came back to it.

Almost all of them were private, and almost all of them are gone. The ones we still read — Milton’s, Locke’s, Jefferson’s, Auden’s — survived because somebody eventually digitised them and let the rest of us in. Reading another person’s is a peculiar pleasure: not their argument, just the shape of their attention.

So this one starts public. If a commonplace book is where you keep the things that strike your fancy, keeping it in the open is the only part that costs nothing and changes everything: it lets somebody else fall down the same holes.

Lastly, I confess that I have as vast contemplative ends, as I have moderate civil ends: for I have taken all knowledge to be my province.

Francis Bacon to Lord Burghley, 1592 · paperlanterns.ink

How to read this book

Two inks, and only one of them is me.

Every folio here is layered: somebody else’s words, and then my reaction to them. The page keeps those two things apart with colour, and the rule is narrower than it looks — red marks whose voice it is, never where a link goes.

Black is the world: the quote, the poem, the other person’s title, the domain it came from, the date, the count. Red is me: my reaction, the heads I chose to file things under, and every offer to go further into the garden. Which means a link in my own voice stays red even when it leaves for somebody else’s site — the voice wins over the destination.

China derangement syndrome

The reason could be that when you are powerful, people take note only when that power falters. The world was hardly impressed when Trump went on his tariff rampage; what struck people was when China successfully fought back.
  • The worldthe headline, the quote, the date, the domain. None of it is mine; I only carried it here.
  • The collector“True dat” — two words, and the whole reason the folio exists. It carries the link out to the FT and stays red anyway.

Most folios are like that one: terse. The hand is usually two to five words, sometimes one. And plenty of folios have no hand at all — something was worth keeping, and there was nothing to add. On those pages red simply does not appear, which is the point. It never holds the layout up.

What is in here

Kept since August 2025.

  • 190 folios, oldest first — the numbering never resets
  • 5 kinds: musings, links, reflections, verse, practical
  • 10 heads, promoted by hand from 143 tags

The heads are chosen, not counted. A tag is what I typed in a hurry; a head is a decision that a subject is worth navigating by, and there are only about ten of those. That is how heads worked in the old books too — the index was an editorial act, not an inventory.

Colophon

Three faces, doing three jobs.

  • Bodoni Moda The display hand: titles, folio numerals, and my own reactions. A didone is severe and a little theatrical, which is right for a book that is mostly other people’s best lines. Its optical size is pinned low so the hairlines survive the dark.
  • Newsreader Everything you actually read. Warm, slightly narrow, built for screens, and its italic is good enough to carry a whole strapline — which is why the sentence under the title is a sentence and not tracked-out capitals.
  • Spline Sans Mono Labels, dates, counts, the index. Machinery should look like machinery. It runs on its own three sizes — forcing UI labels onto the display scale is what makes an editorial page feel like a dashboard.

Built with Astro, served from Cloudflare, written with a good deal of help from Claude. No analytics, no newsletter modal, no cookie banner. The flourishes — the fleuron, the manicule, the asterism — are the devices the old commonplace books used, and they are here for the same reason: to mark a seam without drawing a box.
